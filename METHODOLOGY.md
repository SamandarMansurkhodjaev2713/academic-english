# Academic Communication Learning System (ACLS)

**Design rationale · Version 1.0 · Prepared 5 November 2026**

ACLS is the **name of this course's integrated instructional design**, not the name of a validated, independent, published teaching method. It combines evidence-informed principles into a coherent, job-specific 30-day sequence for an adult who is already around B1 and works in the Academic Support Department of an international university.

## 1. Performance target

The learner should become more accurate, prompt and confident when introducing colleagues, making requests, answering student enquiries, explaining procedures, managing uncertainty and reporting academic coordination actions. These are observable **workplace tasks**, not promises about the outcome of a standardised language examination. Four outcomes for every lesson are formulated as concrete communicative acts.

### CEFR alignment

The Council of Europe's **CEFR Companion Volume (2020)** expands the description of language activity beyond isolated receptive/productive skills to include **interaction, online interaction and mediation**. An Academic Support employee routinely mediates information: for example, reformulating a policy for a confused student while accurately forwarding a request to an authorised programme team. Such performance is more relevant than memorising academic terms without being able to use them.

## 2. Core learning loop

**Understand → Notice → Practice → Produce → Apply → Review**.

1. **Understand:** receive a meaningful workplace scenario, stakeholder and outcome.
2. **Notice:** study a small number of useful word combinations and functional patterns.
3. **Practice:** read and listen to realistic academic contexts, followed by focused comprehension and grammar.
4. **Produce:** independently say or write a response without initially seeing the full model answer.
5. **Apply:** complete a job-specific workplace challenge and self-review using concrete criteria.
6. **Review:** revisit words after spaced intervals, repeat mistakes and retrieve useful phrases without the prompt.

### Why not a grammar-first syllabus?

Grammar is important, but isolated rule memorisation may fail to transfer into conversations. In Day 1, the Present Simple occurs inside introductions; on Day 17, hedging occurs inside diplomatic follow-up emails; on Day 27, reported speech is used for meeting notes. The objective is to use grammar for a communicative purpose.

### Task-Based Language Teaching and communicative practice

Each day ends with a professional objective: coordinate a workshop in another time zone, refer a student’s complaint appropriately, respond to an international partner or produce decision notes. The learner must decide **what to say**, **to whom**, **with what degree of certainty**, and **what happens next**. Tasks progressively shift from predictable to less predictable communication.

## 3. Vocabulary design and repetition

Each day introduces **ten explicit vocabulary items** and six functional phrases. A lexical item may be a phrase (e.g. *point of contact*) rather than a single word. Context, a Russian translation, a short English definition and a sentence help build a network of meaning.

The browser trainer supports **active recall**: initially, only one side of the card is visible. The learner attempts to retrieve the other side, then evaluates their recall as Again, Hard, Good or Easy. This updates repetitions, ease and interval. This is a **bounded adaptation inspired by SM-2**, not a validated prediction of personal memory states. Later reviews are scheduled using the learner’s local calendar date and the historical ratings. A separate difficult-word list collects items marked difficult.

### Evidence for spaced practice

Kim & Webb (2022), *The Effects of Spaced Practice on Second Language Learning: A Meta-Analysis*, synthesised 48 experiments and reported benefits of spaced practice for second-language learning, particularly delayed retention. The specific interval pattern in this app is an implementation choice, not an interval schedule validated by that paper.

### Four strands

The course draws on Paul Nation’s four-strands framing: meaning-focused input, meaning-focused output, language-focused learning and fluency development. The actual daily allocation is **weighted towards job-specific speaking, listening and writing**, not mechanically divided into four exact equal blocks.

## 4. Guidance for adult learners

Start with known workplace tasks at manageable difficulty, add authentic communicative functions, and increase independent production. The first module establishes conversational routines and clarification strategies; later modules cover varied institutional workflows and unexpected problems. The wording is “International English”: broadly intelligible professional wording, with institution-specific terms explicitly signalled as variable.

Linguistic expectations evolve from accurate short sentences and polite questions towards longer explanations, practical hedging, meeting participation and respectful disagreement. A learner should not be penalised merely for using a correct alternative formulation in an open response.

## 5. Feedback model

### Deterministic exercises

Machine-graded items have stable IDs, explicit correct choices and explanations. Blank-input checks normalise punctuation and letter case. Matching requires all four pairs; sentence building compares a constructed full phrase to its reference. When a mistake occurs, the learner sees an explanation, and the attempt is preserved. The learner may retry.

### Open speaking and writing

These are deliberately **not machine-scored**. The learner must produce a substantive response before revealing a model. They then use criteria such as relevance, factual caution, next steps, politeness and readability. Completion is stored as “self-reviewed practice”, not as proof of accuracy or pronunciation. Browser speech recognition, when available, is a transcription aid only.

### Diagnostic vs. final

Placement (17 selected-response items) and final (18) use distinct question selections spanning real course content. The percentages are about those samples, **not a CEFR certification or reliable four-skill proficiency estimate**. Day 1 and Day 30 contain separate self-reviewed spoken/written tasks. Their scores are never rolled into unsubstantiated numerical speaking percentages.

## 6. Academic accuracy and ethics

The course repeatedly distinguishes: *admission* from *enrolment*, *transcript* from *certificate*, *requesting an exception* from *approval*, and *coordinating arrangements* from *signing partnership commitments*. Cases are fictional, neutral and avoid implying that academic offices universally follow a single procedure. The learner is taught to verify policies, consent and decision-making authority rather than guess.

## 7. Limitations of the method

- The initial B1 level is **user-reported**, not independently verified.
- A month of self-study may substantially improve specific work skills, but the amount varies by individual and cannot guarantee formal B2 competence.
- Automated checks cover constrained responses; free-form professional communication requires human feedback for confident objective assessment.
- Synthesis voices are not the same as real audio from different people and accents.
- A high practice percentage can reflect familiarity with known questions rather than general language ability; real-world role-play and new tasks are essential.
